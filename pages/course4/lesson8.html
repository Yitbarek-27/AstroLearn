🌌 Galaxy Groups, Clusters & the Cosmic Web

Galaxies are not scattered randomly through the universe. They are organized into enormous structures that range from small groups of galaxies to massive clusters and gigantic filaments extending across hundreds of millions of light-years. On the largest scales, these structures form a pattern known as the cosmic web. In this final lesson of the Galaxies course, you will explore how galaxies are grouped together, how gravity creates large-scale structures, what galaxy clusters contain, how the Milky Way fits into this enormous network, and how astronomers map the structure of the universe.

🌌 Galaxies Are Part of Larger Structures

A galaxy is an enormous system containing stars, gas, dust, dark matter, and often a supermassive black hole near its center.

However, galaxies are not usually isolated from everything else in the universe.

Gravity causes galaxies to gather into larger structures.

Some galaxies exist in relatively small groups containing a few large galaxies and many smaller ones.

Much larger collections of galaxies are called galaxy clusters.

Groups and clusters can themselves be connected to even larger structures.

When astronomers study the universe on extremely large scales, they find a complex network of galaxies and matter rather than a completely random distribution.

👥 What Is a Galaxy Group?

A galaxy group is a relatively small collection of galaxies that are gravitationally associated with one another.

Galaxy groups can contain several large galaxies along with numerous smaller galaxies.

The galaxies in a group move through space under the influence of their combined gravitational field.

Their interactions can affect their shapes, gas supplies, and rates of star formation.

Galaxy groups are important because they represent one of the common environments in which galaxies evolve.

Our own Milky Way is part of a galaxy group known as the Local Group.

🏠 The Local Group

The Local Group is the collection of galaxies gravitationally associated with the Milky Way and Andromeda Galaxy.

It contains dozens of known galaxies, although most are much smaller than the Milky Way and Andromeda.

The Milky Way and Andromeda are the two largest galaxies in the Local Group.

Several smaller satellite galaxies orbit or interact with the larger galaxies.

The Local Group is therefore an excellent nearby example of how galaxies exist within a larger gravitational environment.

Studying the Local Group allows astronomers to investigate galaxy formation and evolution relatively close to Earth.

🌠 Satellite Galaxies

A satellite galaxy is a smaller galaxy that is gravitationally associated with a larger galaxy.

The Milky Way has several known satellite galaxies.

Some of them are dwarf galaxies containing far fewer stars than the Milky Way.

Satellite galaxies can orbit their larger host galaxies for extremely long periods.

Gravitational interactions can gradually change their structures.

In some cases, material can be pulled from a satellite galaxy, creating long streams of stars around the larger galaxy.

🌀 What Is a Galaxy Cluster?

A galaxy cluster is a much larger gravitationally bound structure containing many galaxies.

Some clusters contain hundreds or even thousands of galaxies.

The galaxies are not the only matter in a cluster.

Clusters also contain enormous amounts of hot gas and dark matter.

The total mass of a cluster can be enormous, making galaxy clusters some of the largest structures that are strongly bound together by gravity.

Their enormous mass makes them important laboratories for studying gravity, dark matter, galaxy evolution, and cosmology.

🔥 The Hot Gas Between Galaxies

The space between galaxies inside a cluster is not completely empty.

Galaxy clusters contain extremely hot, thin gas known as intracluster gas.

The gas can reach temperatures of millions of degrees.

At these temperatures, the gas emits large amounts of X-ray radiation.

X-ray telescopes can therefore detect galaxy clusters even when some of their galaxies are relatively faint.

The properties of this hot gas also help astronomers estimate the amount of matter contained within a cluster.

🌑 Dark Matter in Galaxy Clusters

A large fraction of the mass of a galaxy cluster is thought to consist of dark matter.

Dark matter does not emit or reflect ordinary light in a way that allows it to be directly observed.

However, its gravitational influence can be measured.

The motions of galaxies within clusters can reveal that there is much more mass present than can be explained by visible matter alone.

This provides important evidence for large amounts of unseen matter.

🔭 Gravitational Lensing by Clusters

Galaxy clusters are so massive that their gravity can bend the paths of light traveling near them.

This phenomenon is called gravitational lensing.

When light from a distant galaxy passes around a massive cluster, the cluster's gravity can change the apparent position, shape, or brightness of the background galaxy.

Strong gravitational lensing can sometimes produce arcs or multiple images of distant galaxies.

Weak gravitational lensing causes smaller distortions that can be measured statistically across many background galaxies.

Lensing is one of the most powerful methods for mapping dark matter because gravity responds to both visible and invisible mass.

🌌 Clusters as Cosmic Laboratories

Galaxy clusters contain many different physical environments within a relatively concentrated region.

Astronomers can study galaxies of different ages, sizes, colors, and shapes inside the same large structure.

They can also investigate the hot intracluster gas and the distribution of dark matter.

Comparing galaxies inside clusters with galaxies in less dense environments helps scientists understand how surroundings can affect galaxy evolution.

For this reason, galaxy clusters are important natural laboratories for astrophysics.

🧩 Groups, Clusters, and Superclusters

The universe contains structures at many different scales.

Individual galaxies can belong to groups.

Larger collections form galaxy clusters.

Groups and clusters can be associated with even larger structures known as superclusters.

Superclusters are enormous regions containing many galaxy groups and clusters.

Unlike individual galaxies and many clusters, superclusters are not necessarily gravitationally bound together as one permanent structure.

Instead, they are large-scale concentrations of galaxies and galaxy systems within the cosmic web.

🕸️ What Is the Cosmic Web?

On the largest observable scales, matter in the universe forms a vast network known as the cosmic web.

Galaxies tend to gather along enormous structures called filaments.

These filaments connect dense regions containing galaxy groups and clusters.

Between these structures are enormous regions containing far fewer galaxies, known as cosmic voids.

The result is a pattern resembling a gigantic three-dimensional web extending across the universe.

The cosmic web developed through gravity acting on matter over billions of years.

🧵 Cosmic Filaments

Cosmic filaments are long, relatively dense structures within the cosmic web.

They contain galaxies, gas, and dark matter.

Filaments can connect massive clusters across enormous distances.

They are not solid structures like bridges or cables.

Instead, they are regions where matter is more concentrated than in the surrounding universe.

Their formation is a natural consequence of gravitational growth from the early universe.

⬛ Cosmic Voids

Cosmic voids are enormous regions containing relatively few galaxies compared with the dense structures of the cosmic web.

They are not completely empty.

Very diffuse matter and some galaxies can still exist within voids.

However, the density of matter is much lower than in filaments, groups, and clusters.

Voids can occupy a significant fraction of the volume of the observable universe.

Their existence helps demonstrate that matter is distributed very unevenly on large cosmic scales.

🌊 How Gravity Builds the Cosmic Web

The cosmic web began with small differences in the distribution of matter in the early universe.

Gravity amplified these differences over time.

Matter moved toward regions with greater density.

As matter gathered, the gravitational attraction became stronger.

This process produced sheets, filaments, knots, and voids in the large-scale distribution of matter.

Galaxies formed within this evolving environment and became concentrated in the denser regions.

🌑 Dark Matter Creates the Framework

Dark matter is especially important in models of cosmic large-scale structure.

Because dark matter interacts gravitationally, it responds to the distribution of matter and helps form large gravitational structures.

Ordinary matter is then influenced by the gravitational potential created by this structure.

Computer simulations of the universe show that dark matter can form an enormous network of filaments and dense regions.

Galaxies develop within this larger framework.

🌌 Galaxies Follow the Large-Scale Structure

Galaxies tend to be more common in dense regions of the cosmic web.

Large clusters often occur where several filaments intersect.

Smaller groups can form along filaments or in less dense regions.

This means that the environment surrounding a galaxy can tell astronomers something about the larger structure of the universe.

Studying the distribution of galaxies therefore provides a way to map the cosmic web.

🔭 How Do Astronomers Map the Universe?

Astronomers can create large-scale maps of the universe by measuring the positions and distances of galaxies.

They collect observations from large surveys containing thousands, millions, or even more galaxies.

Each galaxy provides a point in a three-dimensional map.

When enough galaxies are mapped, patterns such as clusters, filaments, walls, and voids become visible.

These maps allow scientists to investigate the structure and evolution of the universe on enormous scales.

📏 Measuring Cosmic Distances

Mapping the universe requires astronomers to estimate how far away galaxies are.

One important tool is the redshift of a galaxy's light.

As the universe expands, light from distant galaxies is stretched toward longer wavelengths.

This shift can be measured in a galaxy's spectrum.

Under appropriate cosmological models, redshift can be related to distance and the time at which the light was emitted.

Combining position on the sky with distance estimates allows astronomers to construct three-dimensional maps.

🔴 Galaxy Redshift

Redshift describes the shifting of spectral features toward longer wavelengths.

In cosmology, the redshift of a distant galaxy is strongly connected to the expansion of the universe.

The greater the cosmological redshift, generally, the farther back in cosmic history we are observing.

Astronomers use spectroscopy to measure precise redshifts.

Large galaxy surveys can therefore combine enormous numbers of redshift measurements to reveal the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies.

📊 Galaxy Surveys

A galaxy survey is a systematic astronomical project designed to observe and catalog large numbers of galaxies.

Surveys can measure galaxy positions, brightness, colors, spectra, redshifts, and other properties.

Modern surveys use large telescopes, sensitive cameras, spectrographs, and powerful computing systems.

By combining data from many observations, astronomers can study the distribution of galaxies across huge regions of space.

These surveys are essential for studying the cosmic web and testing models of cosmology.

🧠 What Can Galaxy Distribution Tell Us?

The way galaxies are distributed contains information about the history and composition of the universe.

If a model predicts a particular pattern of large-scale structure, astronomers can compare that prediction with observations.

Differences between predictions and observations can reveal problems with a model or suggest that additional physical processes need to be considered.

Galaxy clustering is therefore not just a map-making exercise.

It is also a powerful tool for testing ideas about dark matter, dark energy, gravity, and cosmic evolution.

🌠 Galaxy Clusters and the Expansion of the Universe

The universe is expanding, meaning that on very large scales distant galaxies generally become separated from one another as space expands.

However, gravity can keep smaller systems such as galaxies and galaxy groups bound together.

The expansion of the universe therefore does not simply pull apart every structure.

Bound structures can remain together while the space between much larger structures expands.

Understanding this distinction is important when studying the cosmic web.

🌌 The Observable Universe

The cosmic web extends across an enormous region of the observable universe.

The observable universe is the portion of the universe from which light or other information has had time to reach us.

Because the universe has a finite age and light travels at a finite speed, we cannot observe every part of the universe directly.

The observable universe contains an enormous number of galaxies distributed through groups, clusters, filaments, and voids.

Studying these structures helps astronomers investigate how the universe developed from its early state into the complex structure we see today.

🕸️ The Cosmic Web and Galaxy Evolution

A galaxy's environment can influence its evolution.

Galaxies inside dense clusters can experience frequent gravitational interactions.

Their gas can also interact with the hot intracluster medium.

These environmental effects can change star formation and galaxy structure.

Galaxies in lower-density environments can experience different evolutionary histories.

This means galaxy evolution is connected not only to what happens inside a galaxy but also to where the galaxy lives within the cosmic web.

🤝 Galaxy Mergers in Groups and Clusters

The gravitational environment of a group or cluster can affect the frequency and type of galaxy interactions.

Galaxies in smaller groups can move relatively slowly compared with galaxies inside massive clusters.

This can make strong gravitational interactions and mergers possible under suitable conditions.

In massive clusters, galaxies often move at very high speeds relative to one another.

Direct mergers can therefore be less common in some cluster environments, although gravitational interactions still occur.

The environment can therefore influence the evolutionary history of galaxies.

🌟 Why Galaxy Clusters Contain Older Galaxies

Many galaxies in dense cluster environments have relatively low current rates of star formation.

Several environmental processes may contribute to this pattern.

Interactions with hot intracluster gas can remove or disturb gas within galaxies.

Gravitational interactions can also alter galaxy structure.

These processes can reduce the amount of cold gas available for producing new stars.

As a result, many cluster galaxies are dominated by older stellar populations.

🔬 Studying the Cosmic Web With Multiple Wavelengths

Different forms of electromagnetic radiation reveal different parts of large-scale cosmic structures.

Visible light allows astronomers to observe galaxies and their stars.

Infrared observations can reveal dust and cooler objects.

Radio observations can trace certain gas structures and energetic processes.

X-ray observations reveal extremely hot gas inside galaxy clusters.

Combining these observations allows astronomers to investigate both galaxies and the material between them.

💻 Simulating the Cosmic Web

Scientists use computer simulations to reproduce the growth of large-scale cosmic structure.

These simulations begin with conditions representing the early universe.

Gravity then causes matter to gather and form increasingly complex structures.

Over time, simulated matter develops into patterns containing dense regions, filaments, and voids.

Scientists can compare these simulated structures with maps created from astronomical observations.

Agreement between simulations and observations provides important evidence that our models of cosmic structure are capturing major physical processes correctly.

🌑 The Cosmic Web Is More Than Visible Galaxies

When we look at an image of a galaxy cluster, we mostly see starlight from galaxies.

However, the visible galaxies represent only one component of the larger structure.

There is also hot gas between galaxies and a much larger distribution of dark matter.

The cosmic web therefore contains several interacting components.

Understanding the universe requires studying all of these components rather than focusing only on visible stars.

🚀 Why the Cosmic Web Matters

The cosmic web connects the physics of individual galaxies to the evolution of the entire universe.

Its structure contains clues about dark matter and the growth of cosmic density variations.

Galaxy clusters provide opportunities to study extreme gravitational environments.

Cosmic voids show where matter is relatively underdense.

Filaments reveal the pathways along which matter is organized across enormous distances.

By studying all of these structures together, astronomers can investigate how the universe developed over billions of years.

🔭 The Future of Large-Scale Astronomy

Future astronomical surveys will map even larger numbers of galaxies with greater precision.

Improved telescopes will allow astronomers to detect increasingly faint and distant galaxies.

Better measurements of galaxy positions, distances, and properties will create more detailed maps of the cosmic web.

Scientists will compare these observations with increasingly sophisticated simulations.

These studies can help answer important questions about dark matter, dark energy, galaxy formation, and the history of the universe.

The future of astronomy will therefore continue to connect observations of individual galaxies with the largest structures in existence.

💡 Key Idea

Galaxies are organized into larger structures rather than being randomly distributed throughout the universe.

Small collections are called galaxy groups, while much larger gravitationally bound systems are galaxy clusters.

Clusters contain galaxies, enormous quantities of hot gas, and large amounts of dark matter.

On even larger scales, galaxies and galaxy clusters are arranged along filaments and sheets separated by enormous cosmic voids.

This enormous network is called the cosmic web.

The cosmic web developed through gravity acting on small density differences in the early universe.

By mapping galaxies and measuring their distances, astronomers can reconstruct this large-scale structure and use it to test models of how the universe evolved.

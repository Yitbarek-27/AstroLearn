📈 The Expanding Universe

The universe is not static. On the largest scales, space itself is expanding, causing distant galaxies to become separated from one another over cosmic time. In this lesson, we will explore how astronomers discovered cosmic expansion, how galaxy redshift reveals motion, how Hubble's law connects distance and recession velocity, and how these observations allow scientists to investigate the history and evolution of the universe.

⏱ Duration 25–30 minutes
📚 Difficulty Intermediate

🌌 What Does It Mean That the Universe Is Expanding?

When astronomers say that the universe is expanding, they do not mean that galaxies are simply flying outward from one special point into empty space. Instead, on very large scales, the distances between gravitationally unbound galaxies increase because the fabric of space itself is changing.

A useful analogy is a sheet of paper with dots drawn on it. Imagine that the sheet could stretch uniformly. As the sheet expands, every dot becomes farther from the other dots. There is no single dot that must be the center of the expansion within the sheet.

The analogy is not perfect because the real universe has three dimensions of space, and cosmic expansion involves spacetime rather than a physical sheet. Nevertheless, it helps illustrate an important idea: galaxies can become separated because the space between them expands.

Objects that are gravitationally bound do not necessarily expand with the universe. For example, stars inside a galaxy remain held together by gravity. The Earth does not expand because of cosmic expansion, and the Solar System does not grow apart because of the expansion of the universe.

Cosmic expansion becomes especially important when astronomers study enormous distances between galaxies and galaxy clusters.

🔭 How Did Astronomers Discover Expansion?

The idea that the universe might be changing dramatically over time became strongly supported by observations of distant galaxies during the twentieth century.

Astronomers noticed that the spectral lines of many distant galaxies were shifted toward longer wavelengths. This phenomenon is called redshift.

The farther away many galaxies appeared to be, the larger their observed redshifts tended to be.

Astronomers also measured distances to galaxies using several independent methods. When these distance measurements were compared with galaxy redshifts, a broad relationship emerged: more distant galaxies generally showed larger recession velocities.

This relationship became one of the most important observational foundations of modern cosmology.

It is commonly called Hubble's law, named after astronomer Edwin Hubble, whose observations helped establish the relationship between galaxy distance and recession velocity.

🌈 What Is Redshift?

Light behaves like a wave and can be described by its wavelength and frequency. Visible light contains different wavelengths corresponding to different colors.

When the light from a distant galaxy is observed, astronomers can compare known spectral lines from atoms with the positions of those same lines in the galaxy's spectrum.

If a spectral line appears at a longer wavelength than its laboratory wavelength, it is shifted toward the red end of the visible spectrum. This is called redshift.

For relatively small velocities, a simple approximation relates redshift to velocity:

z ≈ v / c

where:

  • z = redshift
  • v = recession velocity
  • c = speed of light

The speed of light is approximately:

c = 300,000 km/s

This approximation is most useful for relatively small redshifts. At very large cosmological distances, astronomers use the full relativistic and cosmological treatment rather than treating redshift as an ordinary Doppler velocity.

📐 Hubble's Law

One of the most useful relationships in introductory cosmology connects the recession velocity of a distant galaxy with its distance from us.

v = H₀d

Here:

  • v is the recession velocity of the galaxy.
  • H₀ is the Hubble constant.
  • d is the distance to the galaxy.

A commonly used approximate value for the Hubble constant is around 70 km/s/Mpc. Different measurements give somewhat different values, and determining its precise value is an active area of cosmological research.

A megaparsec, abbreviated Mpc, is a unit of astronomical distance.

1 Mpc ≈ 3.26 million light-years

The equation tells us that, on sufficiently large scales, a galaxy twice as far away will generally have approximately twice the recession velocity, within the simple Hubble-law approximation.

🧮 Worked Example: Finding Recession Velocity

Suppose a galaxy is located approximately 100 Mpc away and we use:

H₀ = 70 km/s/Mpc

Using Hubble's law:

v = H₀d

Substitute the values:

v = (70 km/s/Mpc)(100 Mpc)

The Mpc units cancel:

v = 7,000 km/s

So the simple Hubble-law estimate of the galaxy's recession velocity is approximately 7,000 km/s.

🧮 Worked Example: Finding Distance

Hubble's law can also be rearranged to find distance.

d = v / H₀

Suppose a galaxy has an estimated recession velocity of 14,000 km/s and we use:

H₀ = 70 km/s/Mpc

Then:

d = 14,000 / 70

d = 200 Mpc

The simple Hubble-law estimate places the galaxy at approximately 200 Mpc.

🔢 Converting Megaparsecs to Light-Years

Suppose the distance to a galaxy is:

d = 200 Mpc

Since:

1 Mpc ≈ 3.26 million light-years

we calculate:

200 × 3.26 million light-years

= 652 million light-years

So 200 Mpc corresponds to approximately 652 million light-years.

🚀 Are Galaxies Really Moving Away From Us?

In everyday language, astronomers often say that distant galaxies are "moving away" from us. This is useful shorthand, but the deeper explanation involves the expansion of space.

Imagine two distant galaxies embedded in an expanding universe. As the universe expands, the space between the galaxies increases. If the galaxies are not strongly gravitationally bound to one another, their separation can grow even if they have little motion relative to their local surroundings.

This is different from the motion of a spacecraft through space. A spacecraft has a local velocity relative to its surroundings. Cosmic expansion describes the changing large-scale distances between objects.

🌍 There Is No Special Center of Expansion

A common misconception is that the universe must be expanding outward from a central location.

The standard cosmological description does not require a special center within ordinary three-dimensional space.

From the viewpoint of an observer in a sufficiently homogeneous and isotropic universe, distant galaxies can generally appear to recede in all directions.

An observer in another galaxy would also see distant galaxies participating in the expansion around them. This does not mean that every observer is located at a physical center. Instead, the expansion is a property of the large-scale geometry of spacetime.

📏 What Is the Hubble Constant?

The symbol H₀ represents the present-day expansion rate of the universe in the standard Hubble-law approximation.

Its units are usually written as:

km/s/Mpc

These units mean that for every additional megaparsec of distance, the simple Hubble-law estimate increases the recession velocity by approximately the numerical value of H₀ in kilometers per second.

  • At 1 Mpc → about 70 km/s
  • At 10 Mpc → about 700 km/s
  • At 100 Mpc → about 7,000 km/s
  • At 500 Mpc → about 35,000 km/s

These are simplified examples intended to demonstrate the relationship. Real cosmological distance calculations become more complicated at large redshifts because the expansion rate has changed over cosmic history.

⏳ The Expansion Rate Has Changed Over Time

The universe has not necessarily expanded at the same rate throughout its entire history.

The expansion rate depends on the contents of the universe, including matter, radiation, and dark energy.

Early in cosmic history, matter and radiation played a much more important role in determining the expansion behavior. As the universe evolved and became less dense, dark energy became increasingly important in the modern cosmological model.

This means that the simple equation v = H₀d is most useful as an introductory relationship describing the present-day large-scale trend. It should not be treated as a complete description of cosmic expansion at every distance and every point in cosmic history.

🌈 Redshift and Wavelength

Astronomers can quantify redshift using the observed and emitted wavelengths of light.

The redshift can be written as:

z = (λobserved - λemitted) / λemitted

For example, suppose an atomic spectral line normally has a wavelength of 500 nm, but astronomers observe it at 550 nm.

Then:

z = (550 - 500) / 500

z = 50 / 500

z = 0.10

The redshift is therefore 0.10.

For small redshifts:

v ≈ zc

Using c ≈ 300,000 km/s:

v ≈ 0.10 × 300,000

v ≈ 30,000 km/s

This is a simplified calculation. At larger redshifts, cosmologists use a more complete relativistic and cosmological interpretation.

🔭 How Do Astronomers Measure Galactic Distances?

Hubble's law is useful, but astronomers first need reliable information about galaxy distances.

There is no single distance-measuring method that works perfectly for every object in the universe. Instead, astronomers use a collection of techniques known as the cosmic distance ladder.

For relatively nearby stars, astronomers can use parallax. As Earth moves around the Sun, nearby stars appear to shift slightly against more distant background stars.

For more distant objects, astronomers can use objects with known or calibrated brightness properties, such as certain types of variable stars and supernovae.

These measurements help establish distances to galaxies. Those distances can then be compared with redshift measurements to investigate the relationship between distance and cosmic expansion.

💡 Why Is Cosmic Expansion Important?

The discovery of cosmic expansion transformed our understanding of the universe.

If the universe is expanding today, then looking backward in time means that the universe was once smaller, hotter, and denser.

This idea forms one of the foundations of the Big Bang model studied in the previous lesson.

Cosmic expansion also allows astronomers to investigate:

  • the history of the universe
  • the growth of galaxies
  • the distribution of matter
  • the role of dark matter
  • the role of dark energy
  • the geometry of the universe
  • the history of cosmic structure

🌌 Looking Back in Time

Light does not travel instantaneously. Even at the enormous speed of light, it takes time to cross cosmic distances.

When astronomers observe a galaxy one billion light-years away, the light entering the telescope today began its journey approximately one billion years ago, under the simple light-travel-time interpretation.

That means distant galaxies provide a view of the universe's past.

As astronomers observe galaxies at increasingly large distances and redshifts, they can study how galaxies and cosmic structures changed over billions of years.

This is one of the most powerful ideas in observational astronomy: looking farther away also means looking further back in time.

🧠 Expansion vs. Local Motion

Not every observed galaxy participates in cosmic expansion in exactly the same way.

Galaxies have their own local motions caused by gravity. These motions are often called peculiar velocities.

For a nearby galaxy, its local gravitational motion can be comparable to or larger than the apparent recession associated with cosmic expansion.

For sufficiently distant galaxies, the large-scale expansion becomes much more dominant in the observed relationship.

This is one reason why Hubble's law is most useful statistically and on large cosmological scales rather than as a perfect prediction for every individual nearby galaxy.

🛰️ Modern Observations of Cosmic Expansion

Today, astronomers measure cosmic expansion using many different observations. These include galaxy redshift surveys, supernova observations, measurements of the cosmic microwave background, gravitational lensing, galaxy clustering, and other cosmological probes.

Different techniques can provide information about different periods in cosmic history.

Scientists compare these observations with mathematical models of the universe. When several independent observations agree with a model, confidence in the model increases.

At the same time, differences between measurements can reveal unresolved questions. The exact present-day expansion rate is one example where different observational approaches have produced values that do not all perfectly agree.

Find its approximate distance.

d = 21,000 / 70

d = 300 Mpc

A spectral line normally appears at 600 nm, but astronomers observe it at 660 nm.

Calculate the redshift.

z = (660 - 600) / 600

z = 60 / 600

z = 0.10

⚠️ Important Limits of Simple Hubble's Law

The equation v = H₀d is extremely useful for learning the basic relationship between distance and cosmic expansion, but real cosmology is more complicated.

The expansion rate has changed over cosmic history. The universe contains matter, radiation, and dark energy, and their relative importance changes with time.

At high redshifts, astronomers cannot simply multiply distance by a constant Hubble value and interpret the result as an ordinary velocity.

Cosmologists instead use models describing how the scale factor of the universe changes with time.

🔑 Key Idea

The universe is expanding on large scales, meaning that the distances between gravitationally unbound galaxies generally increase with cosmic time.

Galaxy spectra provide evidence for this expansion through redshift. Hubble's law gives a simple relationship between recession velocity and distance.

This relationship allows astronomers to connect observations of distant galaxies with the large-scale behavior of the universe.
